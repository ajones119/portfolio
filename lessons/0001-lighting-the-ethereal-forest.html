Light the forest in layers

The forest does not get its mood from one magic light. It comes from five layers with separate jobs. Your win: know which layer to adjust when the image feels flat, harsh, or empty.

The mental model

Warm key

A DirectionalLight('#ffd79a', 4.2) acts as the sun. Its off-axis position produces readable long shadows between trunks.

Cool fill

A HemisphereLight softly colors upward and downward faces differently. It keeps shadowed trees legible without erasing direction.

Shadow pipeline

The renderer enables shadow maps, the sun casts them, trees opt into castShadow, and the ground opts into receiveShadow. Miss any link and shadows disappear.

Atmospheric depth

Fog('#758a80', 18, 78) fades distant trees into the same color as the background. This creates layers without modeling more detail.

Output shaping

ACES filmic tone mapping and toneMappingExposure = 1.08 control how warm highlights and deep greens reach the screen.

Why the shadows stretch

The sun sits at (-28, 34, 18) and aims at the origin. Directional-light rays are parallel, so its side angle creates a consistent diagonal shadow direction across the whole forest. Three.js uses an orthographic camera to decide which region receives those shadows.

sun.castShadow = true;
sun.shadow.mapSize.set(2048, 2048);
sun.shadow.camera.left = -48;
sun.shadow.camera.right = 48;
sun.shadow.camera.top = 48;
sun.shadow.camera.bottom = -48;

The tradeoff: a fixed 2048² map spread across a larger camera area has fewer shadow pixels per world unit. Cover more forest and shadows get less detailed.

Try it in the real scene

  1. Open the forest with #debug.
  2. In Light, lower sun y. Predict the shadow change before looking.
  3. Set sun intensity to zero. Notice that the hemisphere fill preserves shape but loses strong direction.
  4. Restore the values and adjust exposure last.
